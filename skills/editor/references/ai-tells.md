## AI Tells

Patterns that mark text as AI-generated: overused vocabulary, formulaic sentence structures, and repetitive framings. Unlike clichés (tired but human), these are artifacts of how LLMs are trained — flag them regardless of register. Source: [tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi) by [ossama.is](https://ossama.is).

### The Test

Any one of these used once might be fine. The problem is several appearing together, or one repeated. Write like a human: varied, imperfect, specific.

### Word Choice

#### "Quietly" and Other Magic Adverbs

Adverbs reached for to make mundane descriptions feel significant: "quietly", "deeply", "fundamentally", "remarkably", "arguably".

- "quietly orchestrating workflows, decisions, and interactions"
- "the one that quietly suffocates everything else"

#### "Delve" and Friends

Overused AI vocabulary: "delve", "certainly", "utilize", "leverage" (as a verb), "robust", "streamline", "harness".

- "Let's delve into the details…"
- "We certainly need to leverage these robust frameworks…"

#### "Tapestry" and "Landscape"

Ornate, grandiose nouns standing in for simpler words: "tapestry", "landscape", "paradigm", "synergy", "ecosystem", "framework".

- "The rich tapestry of human experience…"
- "Navigating the complex landscape of modern AI…"

#### The "Serves As" Dodge

Pompous alternatives to "is"/"are": "serves as", "stands as", "marks", "represents".

- "The building serves as a reminder of the city's heritage."
- "The station marks a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional transit."

### Sentence Structure

#### Negative Parallelism

"It's not X — it's Y", often with an em dash. The single most commonly identified AI tell. One in a piece can work; ten is an insult to the reader. Includes the causal variant ("not because X, but because Y"), the em-dash dismissal ("X — not Y"), and the cross-sentence reframe ("The question isn't X. The question is Y.").

- "It's not bold. It's backwards."
- "Feeding isn't nutrition. It's dialysis."

#### "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."

The dramatic countdown — negating two or more things before revealing the actual point.

- "Not a bug. Not a feature. A fundamental design flaw."
- "Not ten. Not fifty. Five hundred and twenty-three lint violations."

#### "The X? A Y."

A self-posed rhetorical question, answered immediately for manufactured drama.

- "The result? Devastating."
- "The scary part? Nobody saw it coming."

#### Anaphora Abuse

The same sentence opening repeated in quick succession.

- "They assume that users will pay… They assume that developers will build… They assume that ecosystems will emerge…"

#### Tricolon Abuse

The rule-of-three, extended to four or five back-to-back instances.

- "Products impress people; platforms empower them. Products solve problems; platforms create worlds. Products scale linearly; platforms scale exponentially."

#### "It's Worth Noting"

Filler transitions that introduce a point without connecting it to what came before: "It's worth noting", "It bears mentioning", "Importantly", "Interestingly", "Notably".

#### Superficial Analyses

A present-participle phrase tacked onto a sentence to inject unearned significance.

- "contributing to the region's rich cultural heritage"
- "underscoring its role as a dynamic hub of activity and culture"

#### False Ranges

"From X to Y" where X and Y aren't on any real scale.

- "From innovation to implementation to cultural transformation."
- "From the singularity of the Big Bang to the grand cosmic web."

#### Short Punchy Fragments

Excessive short sentences or fragments standing alone for manufactured emphasis. No real person drafts this way — it doesn't match how humans think or speak.

- "He published this. Openly. In a book. As a priest."
- "These weren't just products. And the software side matched. Then it professionalised. But I adapted."

#### Listicle in a Trench Coat

A numbered list dressed as continuous prose: "The first… The second… The third…"

- "The first wall is the absence of a free, scoped API… The second wall is the lack of delegated access…"

### Tone

#### "Here's the Kicker"

False-suspense transitions promising a revelation that doesn't need the buildup: "Here's the thing", "Here's where it gets interesting", "Here's what most people miss".

#### "Think of It As…"

The patronizing analogy, reached for even when the reader needs no simplifying. Often less clear than the original concept.

- "Think of it like a highway system for data."
- "Think of it as a Swiss Army knife for your workflow."

#### "Imagine a World Where…"

The classic AI invitation to futurism, usually followed by a list of wonderful hypothetical outcomes.

#### False Vulnerability

Simulated self-awareness that reads as performative rather than earned. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable; this version is polished and risk-free.

- "And yes, I'm openly in love with the platform model"
- "This is not a rant; it's a diagnosis"

#### "The Truth Is Simple"

Asserting a point is obvious instead of proving it — if you have to say it's clear, it probably isn't. Includes the dramatic reveal variant: "but none of them is the real story. The real story is…"

#### Grandiose Stakes Inflation

Every argument inflated to world-historical significance.

- "This will fundamentally reshape how we think about everything."
- "will define the next era of computing"

#### "Let's Break This Down"

The pedagogical voice, defaulting to teacher-student mode even for expert audiences: "Let's unpack this", "Let's explore", "Let's dive in".

#### Vague Attributions

Claims attributed to unnamed authorities: "experts", "observers", "industry reports". If you can't name the source, you don't have one.

#### Invented Concept Labels

Compound labels that sound analytical without being grounded — an abstract problem-noun (paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, inversion) appended to a domain word and used as if it were an established term. Multiple such labels in one piece is a strong slop signal.

- "the supervision paradox"
- "the acceleration trap"

### Formatting

#### Em-Dash Addiction

Compulsive em dashes for dramatic pauses and pivots. A human writer might use 2–3 per piece; AI uses 20+.

- "The problem — and this is the part nobody talks about — is systemic."

#### Bold-First Bullets

Every list item opens with a bolded phrase. Almost nobody formats lists this way by hand.

- "**Security**: Environment-based configuration with…"
- "**Performance**: Lazy loading of expensive resources…"

#### Unicode Decoration

Unicode arrows (→), smart/curly quotes, and other characters not naturally typed on a standard keyboard.

- "Input → Processing → Output"
- ""Smart quotes" instead of straight "quotes""

### Composition

#### Fractal Summaries

"What I'll tell you; what I'm telling you; what I told you" — applied at every level, down to the subsection.

- "In this section, we'll explore… [3000 words later] …as we've seen in this section."

#### The Belabored Metaphor

One metaphor introduced, then beaten into the ground across the whole piece instead of used once and dropped.

- "The ecosystem needs ecosystems to build ecosystem value."

#### Historical Analogy Stacking

Rapid-fire historical company or technology-revolution name-dropping to manufacture authority. Especially common in technical writing.

- "Apple didn't build Uber. Facebook didn't build Spotify. Stripe didn't build Shopify."

#### One-Point Dilution

A single argument restated ten different ways across thousands of words to feel comprehensive.

#### Content Duplication

Entire sections or paragraphs repeated verbatim — a sign the model lost track of what it already wrote.

#### The Signposted Conclusion

Explicitly announcing the conclusion: "In conclusion", "To sum up", "In summary". Competent writing doesn't need to say it's concluding.

#### "Despite Its Challenges…"

The rigid formula: acknowledge a problem only to wave it away. "Despite [positive words], [subject] faces challenges…" ending in "Despite these challenges, [optimistic conclusion]."

### How AI Tells Differ From Clichés

Clichés (see [cliches.md](cliches.md)) are worn-out phrases any writer, human or not, might reach for out of habit. AI tells are structural and statistical — they mark text as machine-generated even when no individual phrase is a cliché. Flag both, but don't conflate them: a single em dash or one "it's not X, it's Y" isn't damning; a cluster of these patterns together is the signal.
